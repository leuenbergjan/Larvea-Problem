How big a weight difference can my larva experiment detect?

Power analysis for comparing mean larval weight between groups with a one-way ANOVA plus Bonferroni-corrected follow-up comparisons. Change any value and every result updates.

1 · Your design

The number of larvae per group that you will actually weigh at the end. Losses go in the dropout field below, and the page then tells you how many to start with.
If you expect, say, 15 % of larvae to die or pupate before weighing, enter 15. The page then tells you how many larvae to start with so that enough are left to weigh.
How much larvae in the same group naturally differ from their group's average. Example: weights 18, 19, 20, 20, 21, 21, 22, 24 mg → average 20.6 mg, typical distance from it ≈ 1.85 mg, so SD ≈ 1.85.
Where to get it: weigh some pilot larvae (paste them below, or use =STDEV.S() in Excel), or take it from a paper reporting “mean ± SD”. If the paper gives SE instead, SD = SE × √n. The smaller the SD, the smaller the difference you can detect.

The p-value below which you call a result significant. 0.05 is the usual choice.
The chance that your experiment gives a significant result if the effect is real and as big as shown. 80 % is the usual minimum, so 1 in 5 such experiments would still miss the effect. 90 % is safer.
After a significant ANOVA you test which groups differ. Every extra test makes the correction stricter. With 3 groups, “vs control” is 2 tests (control–A, control–B) and “all pairs” is 3 (adds A–B). Only choose “vs control” if you won't compare the treatments with each other, and decide this before the experiment. The page uses Bonferroni. Dunnett's test (vs control) and Tukey's test (all pairs) are slightly more powerful, so the real numbers are a little better than shown.
“Difference” in sections 2, 3 and 5 means the gap between the lightest and heaviest group mean. For your own expected values, use section 4.

How this works & caveats

  • Test assumed: one-way ANOVA on weight, groups of equal size, normally distributed weights with the same SD in every group. Power is computed exactly from the noncentral F distribution (the same method G*Power uses).
  • Effect size: Cohen's f = SD of the group means ÷ within-group SD. The “pattern” setting converts f into an actual max–min weight difference.
  • Follow-up comparisons: t-tests using the pooled error from the ANOVA, with α divided by the number of comparisons (Bonferroni). Dunnett's test (vs control) and Tukey's test (all pairs) are slightly less strict, so these numbers err on the safe side.
  • The SD drives everything. Halving the SD halves the detectable difference, while doubling the sample size only shrinks it by about 30 %. Reducing variation (same instar, same age, same weighing time) is often the cheapest way to gain power.
  • Independence: if all larvae of one group share a container, the containers rather than the larvae may be the real replicates.
